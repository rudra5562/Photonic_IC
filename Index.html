Silicon Photonics — Interactive Field Guide

Waveguides: the circuit traces of light

A copper trace guides electrons across a chip. A waveguide does the same job for photons — a nanometre-scale channel etched into silicon that steers light exactly where a photonic integrated circuit (PIC) needs it. Everything below is the physics, geometry, and fabrication reality that makes that possible.

A photon bouncing down a waveguide core by total internal reflection — the same principle explored interactively below.

01 — the core principle

Total internal reflection

why light stays inside the core

A waveguide only works if the core's refractive index is higher than the cladding around it. Silicon (n ≈ 3.48 at 1550 nm) surrounded by silicon dioxide (n ≈ 1.44) gives an index contrast of Δn ≈ 2.0 — among the highest of any waveguide platform on Earth. That's why silicon photonic waveguides can bend light around corners with radii of just a few microns, while an optical fibre needs millimetres.

Below the critical angle θc = arcsin(n_clad / n_core) ≈ 24.4°, light hitting the core-cladding interface reflects back completely — nothing escapes. Above it, light punches through and radiates away. Drag the slider to find the boundary.

02 — the platform

The SOI wafer stack

silicon-on-insulator, layer by layer

Silicon photonics is built on Silicon-on-Insulator (SOI) wafers — the same substrate family used for advanced logic, which is why photonic circuits can piggyback on mature CMOS fabs. Tap a layer to see what it does.

Silicon device layer
220 nm (industry standard)

This thin top silicon layer is where waveguides are etched. 220 nm became the de-facto standard because it balances tight single-mode confinement at 1550 nm against realistic lithography and etch tolerances — most multi-project-wafer foundries (imec, AMF, GlobalFoundries, TSMC's photonics line) build around it.

03 — geometry

Three ways to shape a core

strip, rib, and slot waveguides

Strip (channel) waveguide — fully etched down to the buried oxide, light confined on all sides by cladding. Strongest confinement, tightest bend radii (~5 µm), but rough etched sidewalls scatter more light — the dominant loss source on this geometry.

Rib (ridge) waveguide — only partially etched, leaving a thin slab connecting the core to the surrounding silicon. The mode sits further from rough sidewalls, so loss drops sharply (<0.1 dB/cm achievable). The slab also lets you dope silicon laterally to build electrodes — this is the standard geometry for phase shifters and modulators.

Slot waveguide — two high-index silicon rails separated by a nanometre-scale low-index gap. Counter-intuitively, the optical field concentrates in that gap due to the electric-field discontinuity at a high-to-low index boundary. Used for sensing and for infusing electro-optic polymers into an otherwise passive silicon platform.

05 — the budget

Where the photons go missing

loss mechanisms every design has to budget for

≈≈

Sidewall roughness scattering

The dominant loss term in strip waveguides. Line-edge roughness from lithography and etch scatters light out of the guided mode — typically 1–3 dB/cm on standard strip waveguides, under 0.5 dB/cm on well-controlled rib geometries.

⤵

Bend loss

Tighter bends leak more light — the mode can't turn the corner fast enough and radiates outward. Below the platform's minimum bend radius (~5 µm on high-contrast SOI), loss rises sharply.

⊗

Absorption

Free-carrier absorption in doped regions (needed for modulators) and two-photon absorption at high optical power both remove photons directly, converting them to heat or carriers.

⋈

Mode mismatch / coupling loss

Every junction — fibre-to-chip, waveguide-to-waveguide, straight-to-bend — where the mode shape changes abruptly bleeds some light into radiation modes.

06 — the toolkit

Building blocks of a PIC

tap each to expand

Grating coupler

Diffracts light vertically between the chip surface and an optical fibre, so light can enter/exit anywhere on the wafer — great for wafer-scale test.

Typical loss: 3–5 dB · wavelength & polarization dependent

Edge coupler

An inverse taper gradually expands the mode so it matches a fibre butt-coupled to the polished chip edge. Broadband and lower-loss than grating couplers, but needs facet polishing/dicing precision.

Typical loss: 0.5–2 dB · broadband

MMI splitter

A short multimode section that relies on self-imaging interference to split (or combine) power between output ports. Robust to fabrication variation and broadband — the workhorse power splitter.

Typical: 1×2 or 2×2, low wavelength sensitivity

Directional coupler

Two waveguides run close enough that light evanescently tunnels between them. The split ratio is set by gap and coupling length — precise, but more wavelength-sensitive than an MMI.

Ring resonator

A closed-loop waveguide builds up resonance whenever the round-trip length equals an integer number of wavelengths. Used as wavelength filters, modulators, and ultra-sensitive refractive-index sensors.

Key metrics: Q-factor, free spectral range (FSR)

Mach-Zehnder modulator (MZM)

Splits light into two arms, phase-shifts one via a doped pn-junction (carrier depletion/injection changes refractive index), then recombines the arms so phase modulation becomes intensity modulation. The core device inside every optical transceiver.

Ge-on-Si photodetector

Silicon is transparent at 1310/1550 nm — useless as a detector there — so germanium is epitaxially grown on-chip to absorb the light and convert it back to an electrical signal.

07 — from pattern to part

Fabrication in three moves

where design rules meet the fab floor

1. Lithography — DUV 193 nm immersion steppers pattern the waveguide layout onto photoresist, using the same tool class as advanced CMOS logic, just with far looser critical dimensions.

2. Etch — reactive-ion etching transfers the pattern into silicon. Sidewall angle and roughness set the loss budget for everything downstream — this is exactly the kind of critical dimension and defect signature that optical and e-beam wafer inspection tools are built to catch before it becomes a yield problem.

3. Cladding deposition — PECVD oxide seals the core, completing the index contrast that makes the waveguide guide light in the first place.

09 — why it matters

Where these waveguides end up

Datacenter interconnects

Co-packaged optics move data between AI accelerator clusters at bandwidths copper can no longer sustain.

LiDAR

Optical phased arrays built from waveguide grids steer laser beams with no moving parts.

Quantum photonics

Waveguides route single photons for entanglement generation and photonic quantum computing.

Biosensing

Ring-resonator sensors detect molecular binding events through tiny refractive-index shifts.
